Secure AI from perception to action

Shaikh Akib Shahriyar

Ph.D. Candidate in Computing and Information Sciences
Rochester Institute of Technology (RIT), Rochester, New York
Graduate Research Assistant, ESL Global Cybersecurity Institute
Advisor: Matthew Wright

My research studies the security of AI systems that perceive and act, from deepfake detectors that analyze multimodal media to agentic AI systems that use tools, retain memory, and act on users’ behalf. I combine adversarial machine learning with systems-oriented, cost-aware evaluation to examine how attackers manipulate models, tools, memory, and multimodal inputs under realistic constraints. My goal is to develop practical evaluations and defenses that preserve legitimate functionality while keeping AI-driven decisions and actions reliable, authorized, and consistent with user intent.

Research

Current work

Practical Robustness Frontier. How much access does an attacker need to evade a deepfake detector? With collaborators, I evaluate twelve detectors from four architectural families across attacker-access levels, from zero-query transfer to adaptive white-box attacks. Working paper

Executable task contracts. When an evaluation edits a tool-using agent’s environment, is it still the user’s original task? Executable contracts make this task-relative validity checkable before the agent’s behavior is observed. Preprint

I also contribute research to usable media-forensics tools for journalists and analysts: VARUNA, supported by NSF, and DeFake, supported by NSF and the Knight Foundation.

Research agenda

My group will pursue four connected directions.

  1. Cost-aware multimodal robustness. Develop practical methods for evaluating the robustness of AI systems that analyze audio, video, text, and provenance signals. Building on my Practical Robustness Frontier—a framework that compares attacks by the access, queries, feedback, and semantic changes they require—I will study when combining multiple forms of evidence actually makes evasion harder and use those findings to design defenses against adaptive attackers.
  2. Task-preserving attacks and secure agent learning. Using executable task contracts to generate attacks that preserve the authorized task and to train agents against them, and studying how learned policies complement runtime checks without sacrificing legitimate task completion.
  3. Secure multi-agent orchestration. Studying how authorization and evidence propagate when agents delegate and collaborate, through scoped delegation and provenance-aware aggregation, since several agents repeating one poisoned source do not provide independent evidence.
  4. Persistent-memory security and personalized agents. Tracing how malicious content survives memory writing, summarization, and retrieval, and developing revocation, quarantine, and repair mechanisms. Longer term: consented personalized agents whose fidelity and permissions can be evaluated.
